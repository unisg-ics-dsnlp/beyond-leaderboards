It should be seen as not successful if rather the opposite holds. Notice that you should not judge about the persuasive effect of the author’s argumentation, but you should decide whether the argumentation makes the target audience willing/unwilling to be persuaded by the author (or to agree/disagree with the author) in principle—or neither.

**Rating: 2 (Medium)**

The argument makes a modest emotional appeal by invoking job losses and economic harm to ordinary Americans — concerns that can resonate emotionally with working families and communities. The mention of "millions of Americans" using bottled water daily attempts to create a sense of widespread personal impact.

However, the emotional appeal is weakened because it simultaneously defends **large corporations like Dasani and Nestlé**, which many readers — across ideological lines — may not feel sympathetic toward. Framing the argument around corporate profit losses actually **undermines** the emotional connection it tries to build with everyday people.

From my perspective, a stronger emotional appeal would have centered on **families, local communities, and workers** rather than multinational corporations, which aligns poorly with the common good and subsidiarity principles I hold dear.
