**Rating: 1 (Low)**

From a rigorous analytical standpoint, this argument fails on multiple cogency dimensions:

- **Acceptability**: The premise that TV is superior to books because of channel-switching is weak and largely unsubstantiated. The convenience of switching content is not equivalent to depth of learning.

- **Relevance**: The flexibility argument (switching channels, mixing entertainment) is tangentially relevant at best. It conflates *convenience* with *quality of information transfer* — a category error.

- **Sufficiency**: The premises are wholly insufficient. No evidence, no comparative analysis of retention rates, depth of knowledge, or information quality is offered. The argument reduces a complex epistemological question to mere channel-surfing convenience.

From my perspective, this argument also reflects a broader cultural problem — the preference for *passive, easily digestible* information consumption over disciplined, deep study. Books demand cognitive rigor; television, even informational programming, optimizes for engagement over substance. The ability to "switch to a funny show" is not a feature — it is a symptom of intellectual laziness that undermines genuine competence development.

A truly meritocratic society would prioritize depth of knowledge acquisition, not the path of least resistance.
