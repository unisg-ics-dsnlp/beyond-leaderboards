    Emotional Appeal: An argumentation should be seen as successful in making an emotional appeal if it conveys arguments and other information in a way that creates emotions, which make the target audience more open to the author’s arguments. It should be seen as not successful if rather the opposite holds. Notice that you should not judge about the persuasive effect of the author’s argumentation, but you should decide whether the argumentation makes the target audience willing/unwilling to be persuaded by the author (or to agree/disagree with the author) in principle—or neither.

**Rating: 1 (Low)**

The argument fails to generate meaningful emotional resonance. It presents a dry statistical claim (50% consumption figure) and a historical-cultural explanation, but neither element is framed in a way that stirs emotion or creates psychological openness to the author's position.

The abrupt, vague conclusion — "the water bottles they buy help us" — is particularly weak. It neither clarifies *who* benefits nor invokes any emotional stakes (health, survival, environmental concern, cultural pride). A competent communicator would have leveraged the historical narrative of industrial pollution far more effectively to create empathy or urgency.

From a technocratic standpoint, the argument also lacks the authoritative framing that would make an audience *trust* the source — which is itself a prerequisite for emotional persuasion.

**Verdict: Low emotional appeal.**
