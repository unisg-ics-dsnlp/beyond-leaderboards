    Credibility: An argumentation should be seen as successful in creating credibility if it conveys arguments and other information in a way that makes the author worthy of credence, e.g., by indicating the honesty of the author or by revealing the author’s knowledge or expertise regarding the discussed issue. It should be seen as not successful if rather the opposite holds. Decide in dubio pro reo, i.e., if you have no doubt about the author’s credibility, then do not judge him or her to be not credible.

**2 (Medium)**

The author cites industry data from the Beverage Marketing Corporation and supplies concrete figures on revenue and volume, which signals some familiarity with the sector and thus partial credibility. Yet from a materialist class perspective this remains limited: the source is a bourgeois consulting firm that tracks commodity expansion without exposing the underlying exploitation—the enclosure of water as private property, the extraction of surplus value from labor, and the alienation of a basic need into profit. Incomplete phrasing (“U.S. alone grew by over 13%”) further weakens the display of rigorous expertise. In dubio pro reo the effort is not wholly unsuccessful, but it falls short of high credibility.
