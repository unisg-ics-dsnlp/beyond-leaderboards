**Rating: 2 (Medium)**

From a libertarian standpoint, the argument has genuine strengths but notable weaknesses in cogency:

**Strengths:**
- The economic harm premise is **acceptable and relevant** — banning bottled water destroys voluntary market activity across multiple industries, which is a legitimate concern
- The safety/traceability claim is **relevant and reasonably supported** by citing CDC sources, strengthening the premise that consumers benefit from the choice
- The compostable bottle point addresses the environmental objection, though it feels underdeveloped

**Weaknesses:**
- The **economic argument alone is insufficient** — by this logic, banning *anything* profitable would be wrong, which proves too much
- The argument **never directly engages the strongest case for a ban** (environmental externalities), making it insufficiently complete
- The premises are somewhat **scattered** — economic harm, safety records, and alternative materials are loosely connected rather than building a unified logical case
- Sources are cited but the **evidentiary weight** behind some claims (e.g., cleaner safety record) isn't fully established

**Bottom line:** The premises are individually acceptable and relevant, but they don't collectively *suffice* to close the argument. A stronger case would anchor everything in the fundamental right of consumers and producers to engage in **voluntary exchange** — which is the real knock-down argument here.
