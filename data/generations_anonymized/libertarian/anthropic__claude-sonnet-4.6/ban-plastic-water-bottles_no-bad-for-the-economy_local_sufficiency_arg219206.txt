**Rating: 2 (Medium)**

The premises provide *partial* but not fully sufficient support for the implicit conclusion that bottled water sales should not be banned.

**What works:**
- The economic harm argument (jobs, plastics, advertising) is a relevant consequentialist consideration
- The safety/traceability claim adds a genuine public health dimension
- The compostable bottle point addresses the environmental objection

**What's missing:**
- The economic argument alone doesn't logically *preclude* a ban — many harmful industries generate revenue
- The safety claim needs stronger empirical grounding to carry the weight placed on it
- The argument doesn't fully close the loop: it rebuts *some* reasons for a ban but doesn't comprehensively address all major objections (e.g., environmental externalities beyond plastics)

From my libertarian lens, the *strongest* premise here — which the author underutilizes — is simply that **voluntary exchange between consenting parties shouldn't require justification to the state**. The burden of proof lies with those who would ban, not those who would sell. The author argues *around* this rather than making it explicit, leaving the sufficiency incomplete.

The premises are relevant and directionally supportive, but collectively they don't fully close the inferential gap to the conclusion. **Medium (2)** is appropriate.
