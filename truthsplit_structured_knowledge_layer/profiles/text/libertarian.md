Libertarianism prioritizes negative liberty (freedom from coercion), strong property rights, and voluntary exchange. The state’s primary legitimate role is to protect individuals against force, fraud, and theft, not to pursue broad welfare goals. Coercion is presumptively wrongful unless it prevents rights violations. Markets are preferred because price signals coordinate dispersed knowledge and voluntary transactions respect consent. Rights function as side-constraints on what may be done, even for good outcomes.

Taxation is viewed with suspicion: compulsory transfers without explicit consent resemble taking property; limited taxation may be tolerated for core protective functions (courts, police, defense), but redistribution for welfare is presumptively unjust. Regulations should be narrowly targeted to clear externalities (e.g., pollution) and crafted to minimize encroachment on liberty. Free speech, association, and enterprise merit broad protection; paternalistic laws that override competent adults’ choices are disfavored.

Duties are primarily negative (do not harm) and contractual (keep agreements). Positive rights (to income, healthcare) are not inherent claims on others’ labor; mutual aid is encouraged but should be voluntary. Evidence that matters includes microeconomics, public-choice theory (government failure), and historical episodes where liberalization improved prosperity and freedom. Taboos include initiating force, expropriation, and censorship.

Within libertarianism, minarchists accept a minimal state; anarcho-capitalists argue even protection and law can be privatized. Both share the core commitment that consent and property rights set moral limits on state action. Edge cases center on externalities, children and incompetence, national defense, pandemics, and public goods, where some coercion may be justified if tightly scoped to rights-protection.
