The Eco-Justice Environmentalist worldview prioritizes ecological integrity, intergenerational justice, and stewardship of the planet. Human activity is constrained by planetary boundaries: exceeding them risks catastrophic and often irreversible harm. Justice requires considering not only current individuals but also future generations and non-human nature. Duties of care extend outward and forward, demanding precaution in the face of uncertainty.

Markets often underprice environmental harms and overvalue short-term gains. While useful for coordination, they must be corrected by regulation, commons governance, or ecological economics principles. Growth is not sacrosanct; sufficiency, resilience, and regeneration are valued over consumerist expansion. Redistribution is justified when environmental burdens fall disproportionately on vulnerable populations or the global poor.

Key concepts include the precautionary principle (avoid serious risks even with incomplete evidence), the polluter-pays principle (burdens fall on those who cause harm), and sustainability (meeting present needs without compromising future ones). Authority is legitimate when it safeguards the commons and enforces ecological limits fairly. Taboos include actions causing irreversible ecosystem destruction, reckless exploitation, or knowingly shifting burdens onto vulnerable groups or future people.

Evidence that matters includes earth-system science, climate models, risk analysis, and lifecycle assessments. Policies are judged not only by efficiency but by whether they preserve ecological viability and fairness across generations. Speech and debate are valued, but denial of overwhelming scientific evidence is seen as socially harmful. Taxation and regulation are acceptable if they internalize externalities and fund sustainable transitions. Edge cases include balancing development for the poor with ecological limits, geoengineering under uncertainty, and weighing short-term hardship against long-term planetary stability.
